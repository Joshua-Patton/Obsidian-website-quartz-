#exposition 
The history of field theory grew primarily from attempts to understand polynomial equations. Renaissance mathematicians such as Scipione del Ferro (c. 1515), Tartaglia, Cardano, and Ferrari discovered solutions of cubic and quartic equations, but the search for an analogous quintic formula eventually failed: Paolo Ruffini (1799) gave an incomplete early argument, while Niels Henrik Abel (1824) proved that the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals. The decisive conceptual advance came from Évariste Galois (1830–1832), who studied the permutations of polynomial roots preserving their algebraic relations and connected these symmetries with the intermediate number systems containing the roots—the origin of Galois theory and the modern relationship between groups and field extensions. In parallel, Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) developed modular arithmetic, while Galois's work on congruences helped establish the theory of what are now called finite fields. During the later nineteenth century, Richard Dedekind developed the concept of a Körper (field) in connection with algebraic number theory, while Leopold Kronecker and others systematically studied algebraic extensions. The subject was finally placed on a broadly modern axiomatic foundation by Ernst Steinitz in his landmark 1910 paper Algebraische Theorie der Körper, which systematically treated characteristic, prime fields, algebraic and transcendental extensions, and algebraic closure. In the 1920s–30s, Emil Artin recast Galois theory in its modern structural form, emphasizing automorphism groups of field extensions and the correspondence between intermediate fields and subgroups. Field theory subsequently became fundamental not only to algebra and number theory but also to algebraic geometry, where fields of rational functions encode geometric information and, eventually, the replacement of fields by general commutative rings helped lead to the modern theory of schemes.
